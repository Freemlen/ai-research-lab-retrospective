# What We Learned

## 1. AI can create research complexity faster than scientific value

The project demonstrated that frontier models can generate:
- derivations;
- simulation code;
- independent-looking analyses;
- provenance systems;
- sealed review packets;
- adversarial reviews;
- literature maps;
- and increasingly elaborate gates.

That capability can become self-reinforcing. A deep workflow is not evidence that the underlying scientific question matters.

## 2. The best moments were when the system killed its own exciting result

The clearest examples were:
- DYN2: an interesting endpoint-flux signal was reproduced in an eta=0 dispersive control and lost its physical interpretation.
- DYN3 Stage B: apparently usable directions were withheld after control failures.
- DYN4: the two-pulse campaign was stopped at T=4 because the numerical admission gate did not close.
- WILD2: the dramatic “Tenet mathematics” framing was mostly reduced to established optimization/control structures.
- ACTIVE0.5: a promising theoretical route was stopped after the proof architecture failed to reduce to a single closeable gap.

These are successes of scientific discipline even though they are not positive physics results.

## 3. Rigor and significance are different axes

Hashes, freeze receipts, repeated reviews, and careful scoping improved reliability.

They did not establish:
- novelty;
- importance;
- external usefulness;
- or publication value.

A perfectly reproducible answer to an unimportant question remains unimportant.

## 4. AI-on-AI review is not independent human validation

Separate agents and fresh Pro contexts are useful for:
- error finding;
- adversarial checking;
- consistency review;
- and forcing explicit assumptions.

They can still share training priors, blind spots, framing errors, and code ancestry.

A serious future research project should obtain an early reality check from a real domain specialist before heavy compute.

## 5. Literature/value checks should happen before heavy computation

The project waited too long before asking:
- Is this already known?
- Who would care?
- What exact result would change a domain expert's belief?
- What would be lost if this question were never pursued?

WILD2/WILD3 should have happened much earlier.

## 6. A blocker is not a negative physical result

Repeatedly important:
- numerical admission failure != physical failure;
- failure to prove != proof of falsehood;
- lack of a candidate != proof no candidate exists;
- control-limited != physics-limited.

Preserving these distinctions prevented several overclaims.

## 7. One narrow claim beats a mega-architecture

The weakest project phases tried to combine:
- novel physics;
- novel optimization;
- new diagnostics;
- multifidelity models;
- surrogate layers;
- provenance machinery;
- and adversarial review.

The strongest phases focused on one question:
- Is endpoint flux a valid retention discriminator?
- Is this shape direction admissible?
- Does this proof architecture actually close?

## 8. Sunk cost must not vote

Billions of tokens, thousands of artifacts, and weeks of work do not increase the probability that the next research stage is worth doing.

Past spend is evidence about process cost, not future scientific value.

## 9. The AI-research workflow itself may be the more interesting story

The most unusual durable story may be:
one person used frontier AI systems to construct an adversarial research workflow in which other agents repeatedly tried to invalidate seemingly interesting results.

That is not yet a controlled study of AI effectiveness, but it is a real case study and a potentially useful public narrative.

## 10. Closure is part of good research

Stopping this branch is not an admission that every idea was worthless.

It is the correct response to a project whose evidential machinery became much larger than its externally demonstrated scientific yield.
